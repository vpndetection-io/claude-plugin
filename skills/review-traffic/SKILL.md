# Review traffic with VPNDetection

1. Find the IP address in each record, and keep whatever identifies the record (user, email, order ID, timestamp) beside it. If a record has more than one address, use the client address, not a server or proxy hop, and say which column you used. If the records carry the client's user agent (a `User-Agent` column, or the quoted agent in an access log line), keep it beside the address too.
2. Look up every distinct address with a single `lookup_ips` call. If you kept user agents, pass them as `user_agents`, one per address, keyed as in `ips`; where one address sent several, pass the one that claims to be a bot. A batch needs a key on a paid plan: on any other key, a list of more than 10 addresses comes back `bad_request` on every address, so say a review that size needs a paid plan and offer to check the 10 records the person cares about most. Read `coverage` first: a category the key's plan does not include was not checked, so never report those records as clean on that category.
3. Join the results back onto the records and sort them into what they are:
   - `is_resproxy`, `is_mobproxy`: residential or mobile proxy. The address looks like a home or phone connection but is resold, which is the strongest signal of the set.
   - `is_vpn`, `is_tor`: a VPN or a Tor node. Deliberate anonymization, common among ordinary privacy-minded users too.
   - `is_dcproxy`, `is_hosting`: a datacenter proxy or a hosting range. Usually automation, scripts or servers rather than a person at a device.
   - `is_pubproxy`: a public proxy, one anyone can use for free, so read it like a datacenter proxy. Where the result carries the `pubproxy` detail, a recent `last_usable` counts for more than the match alone, since most public proxies work only some of the time.
   - `is_relay`: a privacy relay such as iCloud Private Relay, or an enterprise web gateway such as Zscaler: ordinary people with a privacy setting on, or at work behind their company's gateway. Weigh it lightly.
   - `is_cdn`: a CDN range. Usually a service fetching on someone's behalf.
   - `is_ssh`, `is_smtp`, `is_web`, `is_dns_resolver`: an SSH, mail or web server, or an open DNS resolver, answered on the address. A person's own connection rarely runs one, so read it with `is_hosting`: a server, not a person at a device.
   - `is_anycast`: the address is announced from many places at once, as a public DNS resolver's or a CDN's edge is. A person's own connection is not.
   - `is_carrier`, `is_satellite`: a mobile carrier's or a satellite operator's space, named in `carrier.name` or `satellite.provider_name`. Many people share one address there, so several accounts behind one such address is expected, not a pattern. Neither is anonymization.
   - `is_bittorrent`: a BitTorrent client was seen on the address in the last 90 days. Common on home connections; on a hosting range, a seedbox or a server. `bittorrent.indexer` true means a DHT indexer rather than people.
   - `is_bot`: an automated client uses the address, such as a search engine's or an AI company's crawler, a monitor or a scanner. On a paid plan `bot.provider_name` says whose, or whose it claimed to be where it isn't verified, and `bot.identity` how far to trust it: `verified` (the operator vouches for the address), `declared` (it names itself a bot), `spoofed` (it claimed a bot whose operator vouches for other addresses) or `undeclared` (it hides as a browser). A signup or order from one is most likely automation rather than a person, and `spoofed` or `undeclared` is the one to flag.
   - `user_agent`, on a paid plan when you passed an agent: `agent` names what the agent claims to be, and `verdict` whether this address bears it out. `verified` is that crawler for real; `spoofed` is a client forging the agent of a bot whose operator vouches for other addresses, the one to flag; `unverifiable` is a bot no published list can confirm; `not_bot` is a browser, an app or a script. A result without `user_agent` did not check the agent, which is no verdict either way.

   Every result also carries `hostname` (the reverse DNS name), `location` (the country, its continent and timezone, and the city on a paid plan) and `asn` (the network: its number, name, type, country, announced prefix and `rpki_status`), and a paid plan adds `company` (who uses the address: its name, domain and a `type` of hosting, isp, business, education or government). None is a verdict, but each groups the records: a country that doesn't match the account's, an `asn.rpki_status` of `invalid`, one network or company behind many accounts, or an `asn.type` or `company.type` of `hosting` where the key's plan doesn't include `is_hosting`.
4. Report a count per category, then a table of the flagged records with the identifying fields, the address, the category, the provider where known, the agent's verdict where one was checked, and the country, network and company. Then call out patterns that only show up across records: one provider, one network or one address behind many accounts, bursts in time, or several accounts sharing an address.
5. Offer the full joined table as CSV if the person wants to act on it.

Keep the conclusions to what the data shows. A flag says the network is anonymized, not that the person is acting in bad faith, so present the evidence and let the person asking make the call unless they ask you to recommend one.
